📚 WHAT AM I READING?

As the colder weather sets in, I've been spending more time reading. Here's what I've been reading this month:

AGAINST THE MACHINE - PAUL KINGSNORTH

This is an interesting book, I hadn't heard of Paul Kingsnorth before, but I was intrigued by the title and the cover, so I picked it up.

The book is a series of essays that explore the relationship between humans and the natural world, and how in modern society seperating man from nature, this alientates us from our own humanity.

Kingnorth refers to the "machine", a force that is hard to define and describe, but is the matrix of techno-capitalism, industry, and modern society that is driving us away from our natural state, in his own words "Hollowing us out".

The book challenges the reader to consider their relationship with the natural world, and how they can live a more authentic life in a world that is increasingly dominated by the machine.

Kingsnorth's prose is somewhat technically challenging, not on the level of Nieztche or anything, but I'm a fool, so I'll proably need to give it a second read to really engage with the text.

I wouldn't say this book is perfect, it starts out quite strong, and then seems to regress into pot-shots at certain minority groups, while also calling out the anti-christ in section XX.

Regardless of the anti-christ or not, I think the underlying message is still pretty good.

NOWHERE TO HIDE - GLEN GREENWALD

Nowhere to Hide focuses on the story of Edward Snowden, and the NSA surveillance programs that he exposed. The first half of the books discusses the events leading up to Snowden's decision to leak classified information, and the second half of the book focuses on the aftermath of the leaks, including the public reaction and the legal and political consequences for Snowden and his allies.

As far as my actual thoughts on the book, I found it very interesting. In particular, I want to note the balls on Edward Snowden, at only 29 years of age, he was able to make a decision that would change the course of history, and he did so with a clear understanding of the risks involved.

I also want to highlight the techno-surveillence capabilities of the NSA, as even back in 2007, those motherfuckers were all over everything. Phones, emails, internet traffic, you name it, they were monitoring it. It then makes me wonder, "if they were doing this level of surveillance in 2007, with the technology limitations then, wtf are they doing now?".

The final thing I'm thinking is that, one of the hardest parts of large-scale data collection, is the actual analysis of the data. This would have been a problem back in 2007, as they weren't able to leverage LLM's to analyze the data, but now, with the advent of LLM's, they can analyze the data in ways that were previously impossible. This is a scary thought.

I want to dive deeper into some of the topics the book touches on in further articles that I'll publish to my website, but for now, just know that I found the book very interesting, and a very good read!

Thank you to Bean for lending me his copy of the book!

DESIGNING DATA-INTENSIVE APPLICATIONS - MARTIN KLEPPMANN

If you haven't read my 2026 Data Engineering Roadmap, then you may not be aware that as part of my own personal development, I've identified a number of books (listed in the article) as being important for me to read in order to develop my knowledge and skills as a Data Engineer.

One of those books is Designing Data-Intensive Applications by Martin Kleppmann, which I have been reading this month.

I haven't got too far into the book yet, but it's fundamentally about the design of data systems, and how to build applications that can handle large amounts of data. It covers topics such as data modeling, storage, retrieval, and processing, as well as the trade-offs involved in designing data systems.

If you're not into "computers" this book may not be for you, but if you're one of the folks that joined this mailing list through my blog-posts, it'll probably be up your alley!
